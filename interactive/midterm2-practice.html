A. Backward induction finite horizon, deterministic

Q1core2 min

Backward induction is run on a problem with horizon \(T\), terminal cost \(c_\mathrm{T}\) and dynamics \(\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = \mathbf{f}_t(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{u}_t)\). What does it return?

  1. aOne optimal control sequence \(\mathbf{u}_1^\star, \dots, \mathbf{u}_{T-1}^\star\) for the given initial state, like a transcribed NLP
  2. bA cost-to-go \(J_t^\star(\mathbf{x})\) and a decision rule \(\pi_t^\star(\mathbf{x})\) for every stage \(t\) and every state \(\mathbf{x}\); both depend on \(t\) in general
  3. cA single stationary value function \(J^\star(\mathbf{x})\), the fixed point of the Bellman operator, obtained by value iteration
  4. dA local optimum near the initial guess

Two arrays come out: the values (the min) and the policy (the argmin), indexed by stage and state. That is feedback: what to do in every state at every stage, not one plan from one \(\mathbf{x}_1\). With a horizon fixed in advance, the cost-to-go from state \(\mathbf{x}\) depends on how many stages remain, so do not drop the stage index. In the finite-horizon case the procedure is called backward induction; the name value iteration is reserved for successive approximation on a fixed-point equation. Backward induction gives the global optimum when each one-step problem is solved exactly.

Rehearses: what object dynamic programming computes, and why it is stage-dependent.

Q2core7 min, on paper

The states are the integers \(x \in \{0, 1, 2\}\) and the controls are \(u \in \{-1, 0, +1\}\), with dynamics \(x_{t+1} = x_t + u_t\) (a control that would leave \(\{0,1,2\}\) is not allowed). Stage cost \(c(x, u) = 2x + 2|u|\), terminal cost \(c_\mathrm{T}(x) = x\), horizon \(T = 3\), so decisions are made at \(t = 1, 2\).

  1. Write \(J_3^\star\), then compute \(J_2^\star(x)\) and \(\pi_2^\star(x)\) for the three states.
  2. Compute \(J_1^\star(x)\) and \(\pi_1^\star(x)\).
  3. Compare \(\pi_1^\star\) and \(\pi_2^\star\). Why do they differ?
  4. From \(x_1 = 2\), write the optimal trajectory and check its cost against \(J_1^\star(2)\).

(a) \(J_3^\star(x) = x = (0, 1, 2)\). At \(t = 2\), \(J_2^\star(x) = \min_u \{2x + 2|u| + J_3^\star(x+u)\}\). For \(x = 0\): stay, \(0\). For \(x = 1\): down costs \(2 + 2 + 0 = 4\), stay costs \(2 + 1 = 3\): stay, \(J_2^\star(1) = 3\). For \(x = 2\): down costs \(4 + 2 + 1 = 7\), stay costs \(4 + 2 = 6\): stay, \(J_2^\star(2) = 6\). So \(J_2^\star = (0, 3, 6)\) and \(\pi_2^\star = (0, 0, 0)\).

(b) \(J_1^\star(x) = \min_u \{2x + 2|u| + J_2^\star(x+u)\}\). For \(x = 1\): down \(2 + 2 + 0 = 4\), stay \(2 + 3 = 5\): down, \(J_1^\star(1) = 4\). For \(x = 2\): down \(4 + 2 + 3 = 9\), stay \(4 + 6 = 10\): down, \(J_1^\star(2) = 9\). So \(J_1^\star = (0, 4, 9)\) and \(\pi_1^\star = (0, -1, -1)\).

(c) At the last decision, moving costs 2 and saves only one unit of terminal cost, so you stay. With two stages left, moving down at \(t = 1\) lowers \(x_2\) and \(x_3\) by one, which saves 2 on the stage cost at \(t = 2\) and 1 on the terminal cost, more than the price of 2. The number of remaining stages is part of the situation: with a fixed horizon the optimal decision rule depends on \(t\). Stationarity comes with the discounted infinite-horizon case.

(d) \(x_1 = 2 \to x_2 = 1 \to x_3 = 1\): \((4 + 2) + (2 + 0) + 1 = 9 = J_1^\star(2)\).

Q3core3 min

The proof that backward induction is optimal uses a cut-and-paste argument. From state \(\mathbf{x}\) at stage \(t\), an arbitrary sequence costs 11: its first step costs 2 and its tail costs 9, while the optimal tail from the same \(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\) costs 7. Three first steps are available, costing 2, 3 and 1, and leading to states whose optimal tails cost 7, 4 and 8. Which reading is correct?

  1. a\(J_t^\star(\mathbf{x}) = 9\), because the best tail after the first step is 7
  2. b\(J_t^\star(\mathbf{x}) = 1 + 4 = 5\), taking the cheapest first step and the cheapest tail
  3. cCut: the sequence costs at least \(2 + 7 = 9\). Paste: the first steps followed by their optimal tails cost 9, 7 and 9, so \(J_t^\star(\mathbf{x}) = 7\), and every sequence costs at least that much (\(11 \ge 9 \ge 7\))
  4. dThe argument needs gradients of the cost with respect to the first control

Step 1 (cut) gives a lower bound for every sequence: replacing its tail by the optimal tail from the same successor cannot increase the cost. Step 2 (paste) compares first steps, each followed by its optimal tail, and the argmin attains the bound. Option (b) pairs a first step with a tail that starts from a different state. "The proof constructs a solution, so backward induction plays two roles: it is both a proof and an algorithm." No gradients and no constrained solver are involved.

Rehearses: the two inequalities of the cut-and-paste proof.

Q4core2 min

A reservoir system has 4 reservoirs (state dimension \(n = 4\)) and one release per reservoir, over 52 weekly stages. Each storage is discretized into 100 levels. How large is the table that backward induction stores, and how does that compare with transcribing the problem for a single initial state?

  1. aAbout \(52 \times 400\) entries, linear in the dimension
  2. b\(100^4 = 10^8\) values per stage, about \(5 \times 10^9\) in all, each needing its own minimization over releases; a transcription has on the order of \(52 \times (4 + 4) \approx 400\) unknowns but answers for one initial state only
  3. c\(4^{100}\) values per stage
  4. dThe table has the same size as the transcription, because both discretize time into 52 stages

A grid with \(N\) levels per coordinate has \(N^n\) nodes: Bellman's curse of dimensionality. It is the price of asking for an answer everywhere. Trajectory optimization keeps a cost polynomial in the dimension (about \(T(n+m)\) unknowns) by giving up the global, closed-loop answer. Linear interpolation or splines do not remove the exponential count of nodes. Function approximation and Monte Carlo integration are the two levers the rest of the course pulls.

Q5core2 min

The reservoir grid stores one value per 10 hm³. A release sends the storage to 1033.7 hm³, between the nodes 1030 and 1040, where the next-stage values are \(v_{t+1}(1030) = 50\) and \(v_{t+1}(1040) = 60\). Which value does each rule use?

  1. aRounding uses 50 (nearest node, a zero-order hold); linear interpolation uses \(0.63 \cdot 50 + 0.37 \cdot 60 = 53.7\). Both are convex combinations of stored values, unlike a cubic spline, whose cardinal functions have negative lobes
  2. bRounding uses 60; linear interpolation uses 55
  3. cLinear interpolation uses \(0.37 \cdot 50 + 0.63 \cdot 60 = 56.3\)
  4. dBoth rules must call the simulator again at 1033.7

With \(\lambda = (1033.7 - 1030)/10 = 0.37\), \(\hat J_{t+1} = (1 - \lambda)\, v_{t+1,i} + \lambda\, v_{t+1,i+1}\): the closer node gets the larger weight. The nodal representation \(\hat J_t(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_i v_{t,i}\,\ell_i(\mathbf{x})\) with \(\ell_i(\mathbf{x}^{(j)}) = \delta_{ij}\) is the same idea as in collocation, with the nodes placed in the state space instead of on the time axis. Nonnegative weights keep \(\hat J\) within the range of the stored values; a spline can overshoot near a jump, such as the switch of a bang-bang release table, but it is more accurate on smooth functions.

Q62 min

A learned approximation \(\hat J_{t+1}\) is off by the same constant everywhere: \(\hat J_{t+1}(\mathbf{x}) = J^\star_{t+1}(\mathbf{x}) + 40\) for every \(\mathbf{x}\). What happens to the decision rule computed from it at stage \(t\)?

  2. bNothing: the argmin of \(c_t(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{u}) + \hat J_{t+1}(\mathbf{f}_t(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{u}))\) is unchanged by a constant shift; only the ordering the cost-to-go induces over actions matters for the policy

B. LQR and iLQR backward induction over matrices

Q7core8 min, on paper

Scalar LQR: \(x_{t+1} = x_t + u_t\), cost \(\tfrac12 x_T^2 + \tfrac12\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(x_t^2 + u_t^2)\), so \(A = B = Q = R = Q_T = 1\), with \(T = 2\).

  1. Assume \(J_{t+1}^\star(x) = \tfrac12 P_{t+1} x^2\). Write the one-step problem at stage \(t\), set its derivative in \(u\) to zero, and get \(u_t^\star = -K_t x_t\) with \(K_t\) in terms of \(P_{t+1}\).
  2. Substitute back to get the Riccati recursion for \(P_t\) in this scalar case.
  3. Compute \(K_1, P_1, K_0, P_0\). What is the optimal cost from \(x_0 = 2\)?
  4. Run the recursion backward for many stages. To what does \(P_t\) converge, and what is the limiting gain?

(a) \(J_t^\star(x) = \min_u \tfrac12\left[x^2 + u^2 + P_{t+1}(x + u)^2\right]\). Setting the derivative to zero: \(u + P_{t+1}(x + u) = 0\), so \(u_t^\star = -\frac{P_{t+1}}{1 + P_{t+1}}\,x\) and \(K_t = \frac{P_{t+1}}{1 + P_{t+1}}\). This is \((R + B^\top P_{t+1} B)^{-1} B^\top P_{t+1} A\) with every matrix equal to 1.

(b) Plugging in, \(x + u^\star = \frac{x}{1 + P_{t+1}}\), and \(P_t = 1 + K_t^2 + P_{t+1}(1 - K_t)^2 = 1 + \frac{P_{t+1}}{1 + P_{t+1}}\). The general form \(Q + A^\top P A - A^\top P B (R + B^\top P B)^{-1} B^\top P A\) gives the same: \(1 + P - P^2/(1+P)\).

(c) \(P_2 = Q_T = 1\). \(K_1 = 1/2\), \(P_1 = 3/2\). \(K_0 = (3/2)/(5/2) = 3/5\), \(P_0 = 1 + 3/5 = 8/5\). Optimal cost from \(x_0 = 2\): \(\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac85 \cdot 4 = 16/5\). Check: \(u_0 = -6/5\), \(x_1 = 4/5\), \(u_1 = -2/5\), \(x_2 = 2/5\), cost \(\tfrac12\left[4 + \tfrac{36}{25} + \tfrac{16}{25} + \tfrac{4}{25} + \tfrac{4}{25}\right] = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{160}{25} = \tfrac{16}{5}\).

(d) The fixed point solves \(P = 1 + P/(1+P)\), that is \(P^2 - P - 1 = 0\), so \(P_\infty = (1 + \sqrt5)/2 \approx 1.618\) and \(K_\infty = P_\infty/(1 + P_\infty) = 1/P_\infty \approx 0.618\). Far from the end of the horizon the gain is nearly constant; near the end it changes because fewer stages remain. The gains are time-varying in the finite-horizon problem, and no grid or interpolation was needed: the value function stays exactly quadratic.

Rehearses: deriving the Riccati recursion as backward induction specialized to linear dynamics and quadratic costs.

Q8core2 min

Which statement about the finite-horizon LQR solution is correct?

  1. aThe cost-to-go stays exactly quadratic, \(\tfrac12 \mathbf{x}^\top P_t \mathbf{x}\), so backward induction carries one matrix per stage instead of a table; \(R_t \succ 0\) makes the one-step minimization in \(\mathbf{u}\) well posed, and the policy is linear, \(\mathbf{u}_t^\star = -K_t \mathbf{x}_t\)
  2. bThe Riccati recursion runs forward in time from \(P_0 = Q_0\)
  3. cOne needs \(Q_t \succ 0\) strictly; \(R_t = 0\) is fine
  4. dThe gain \(K_t\) depends on the current state \(\mathbf{x}_t\)

"The discrete-time Riccati equation is the Bellman equation, that is, backward induction, specialized to linear quadratic systems." It starts at \(P_T = Q_T\) and runs backward. The minimization in \(\mathbf{u}\) needs \(R_t + B_t^\top P_{t+1} B_t\) positive definite, which \(R_t \succ 0\) guarantees; \(Q_t \succeq 0\) is enough. The gains are computed offline, during the backward pass, and do not depend on the state: the feedback is linear. In code, solve the linear system rather than forming the inverse.

Rehearses: the assumptions and the output of the Riccati recursion.

Q92 min

iLQR is run on the boat-docking problem. Besides a nominal plan \((\bar{\mathbf{x}}_t, \bar{\mathbf{u}}_t)\), it returns gains \(K_t\). The boat is then perturbed by noise during execution and the controller applies \(\mathbf{u}_t = \bar{\mathbf{u}}_t - K_t(\mathbf{x}_t - \bar{\mathbf{x}}_t)\). Which statement is correct?

  1. aThis is MPC, since it uses the measured state
  2. bThis is a local, time-varying feedback controller: it corrects deviations inside a tube around the nominal trajectory where the linearization holds, without re-solving anything; with too much noise the state leaves the tube and the correction fails
  3. cThe gains make the controller globally optimal for the nonlinear system
  4. dThe gains are only used during optimization and are discarded afterward

Each iLQR iteration linearizes the dynamics and quadratizes the cost around the current plan, solves the local LQR problem by a Riccati backward pass, then simulates forward with a line search. The gains are a by-product of that backward pass, and they give feedback for free near the plan. MPC re-solves an optimization from each measured state; iLQR can be used inside MPC, but the tracking controller above is not MPC. DDP has the same structure and keeps the second-order terms of the dynamics that iLQR drops.

Rehearses: what iLQR returns and where its feedback is valid.

C. Stochastic DP and policies expectations, policy classes, ordering

Q10core7 min, on paper

A machine is either good (G) or worn (W). Each period you either run it or repair it, and you maximize the expected total reward over two decisions (\(t = 1, 2\)), with terminal reward 0.

  • Run in G: reward 10; next state G with probability 0.7, W with probability 0.3.
  • Run in W: reward 4; stays W.
  • Repair (in either state): reward \(-1\); next state G.
  1. Compute \(v_2^\star\) and the last-stage decision rule \(\pi_2^\star\).
  2. Compute \(v_1^\star\) and \(\pi_1^\star\). Write the expectation explicitly.
  3. Is the optimal policy stationary? Why is that expected here?
  4. Would randomizing between run and repair in W at \(t = 1\) ever do better? Answer with one inequality.

(a) At the last decision there is no future: \(v_2^\star(\mathrm{G}) = \max(10, -1) = 10\) (run), \(v_2^\star(\mathrm{W}) = \max(4, -1) = 4\) (run).

(b) G: run gives \(10 + 0.7 \cdot 10 + 0.3 \cdot 4 = 18.2\), repair gives \(-1 + 10 = 9\): run, \(v_1^\star(\mathrm{G}) = 18.2\). W: run gives \(4 + 4 = 8\), repair gives \(-1 + 10 = 9\): repair, \(v_1^\star(\mathrm{W}) = 9\).

(c) No: in W the rule is repair at \(t = 1\) and run at \(t = 2\). Repairing pays off only if a period remains to enjoy the good state. With a fixed horizon, the optimal Markov deterministic policy exists but depends on the stage.

(d) No. For any distribution \(q\) over actions, \(\sum_a q(a)\, w(a) \le \max_a w(a)\): a mixture of 8 and 9 is at most 9. Combined with state sufficiency, this is why a Markov deterministic optimal policy exists among all history-dependent randomized ones.

Rehearses: backward induction with an expectation, and why the finite-horizon policy depends on \(t\).

Q11core2 min

Policies can be Markovian or history-dependent, deterministic or randomized (classes MD, MR, HD, HR). Which statement is correct for a finite-horizon MDP with finite states and actions?

  1. aHR policies can do strictly better, because they use more information
  2. b\(\Pi^{MD} \subset \Pi^{HR}\), and the supremum over \(\Pi^{HR}\) is attained by a policy in \(\Pi^{MD}\); that policy is in general nonstationary, one decision rule per stage
  3. cAn optimal policy exists in \(\Pi^{MD}\) and it is always stationary
  4. dRandomized policies are needed whenever the transitions are random

Two facts make it work. State sufficiency: with Markovian dynamics and rewards, the optimal return from a history depends on it only through the current state (backward induction on the history-based equations). And randomizing never beats the best action when maximizing an expectation. The history grows exponentially with \(t\), so restricting to MD rules is what makes the computation possible. Stationarity is a separate question: in the finite-horizon case the rules depend on \(t\).

Rehearses: the policy classes and the reduction to Markov deterministic rules.

Q12core2 min

Two policies on a two-state problem have expected returns \(v^{\pi_A} = (3, 5)\) and \(v^{\pi_B} = (4, 4)\), one entry per initial state. Which is better?

  1. a\(\pi_A\), because its total \(3 + 5\) is larger
  2. b\(\pi_B\), because its worst case is larger
  3. cNeither: under the componentwise partial order the two are not comparable. An optimal policy must be at least as good as both in every state
  4. dThey are equally good, since \(3 + 5 = 4 + 4\)

Optimality presupposes an ordering, and for MDPs it is \(v^{\pi} \ge v^{\pi'}\) in every state. That is a partial order, so two policies can be incomparable; the remarkable fact is that an optimal policy dominates all others simultaneously. Each entry is itself an expectation: taking the mean of the return is a modeling choice that makes the decision maker risk-neutral. Risk-sensitive criteria such as CVaR look at the tail of the return distribution instead.

Q132 min

The inflow to a reservoir network is a 6-dimensional continuous random vector, and the Bellman backup needs \(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}}[J_{t+1}(\mathbf{f}_t(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{w}))]\). You replace the expectation by an average over \(M\) simulated inflows. Which statement is correct?

D. Infinite horizon and policy evaluation discounting, Neumann series, operators

Q142 min

Rewards satisfy \(|r(s,a)| \le 2\) and \(\gamma = 0.95\). Which statement about the discounted value of any policy is correct?

  1. a\(|v^\pi(s)| \le 2/(1 - 0.95) = 40\) in every state, and the same return is obtained, undiscounted, in a modified MDP where every state moves to a zero-reward absorbing state with probability 0.05 at each step

  3. cThe values may be infinite because the horizon is infinite

Q15core7 min, on paper

A fixed policy \(\pi\) on two states, \(\gamma = 0.9\): \[ \mathbf{P}_\pi = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{r}_\pi = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}. \]

  1. Solve \((\mathbf{I} - \gamma\mathbf{P}_\pi)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{r}_\pi\) by hand.
  2. Run three steps of \(\mathbf{v}_{k+1} = \mathbf{r}_\pi + \gamma\mathbf{P}_\pi \mathbf{v}_k\) from \(\mathbf{v}_0 = \mathbf{0}\). How fast does the error in state 1 shrink? Compare with the guaranteed factor \(\gamma\).
  3. Write \(\mathbf{v}^\pi\) as a Neumann series and say why it converges, using \(\|\mathbf{P}_\pi\|_\infty\).
  4. Is \(\mathsf{L}_\pi \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{r}_\pi + \gamma\mathbf{P}_\pi\mathbf{v}\) a linear operator?

(a) State 2 never leaves and earns nothing: \(v(2) = 0.9\,v(2)\), so \(v(2) = 0\). State 1: \(v(1) = 1 + 0.45\,v(1) + 0.45\,v(2)\), so \(v(1) = 1/0.55 = 20/11 \approx 1.818\).

(b) \(\mathbf{v}_1 = (1, 0)\), \(\mathbf{v}_2 = (1.45, 0)\), \(\mathbf{v}_3 = (1.6525, 0)\). Errors in state 1: \(0.818, 0.368, 0.166\), each \(0.45\) times the previous. The guarantee \(\gamma = 0.9\) is a worst case over all states; here state 1 stays put only half of the time, so its error decays at \(\gamma \cdot 0.5\).

(c) \(\mathbf{v}^\pi = \sum_{t \ge 0} (\gamma\mathbf{P}_\pi)^t \mathbf{r}_\pi\). Each row of \(\mathbf{P}_\pi\) sums to one, so \(\|\mathbf{P}_\pi\|_\infty = 1\) and \(\rho(\gamma\mathbf{P}_\pi) \le \|\gamma\mathbf{P}_\pi\|_\infty = \gamma < 1\), which is the condition for the series to converge to \((\mathbf{I} - \gamma\mathbf{P}_\pi)^{-1}\). Here the eigenvalues of \(\gamma\mathbf{P}_\pi\) are \(0.45\) and \(0.9\).

(d) No, it is affine: \(\mathsf{L}_\pi(\mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_2) \ne \mathsf{L}_\pi\mathbf{v}_1 + \mathsf{L}_\pi\mathbf{v}_2\) because \(\mathbf{r}_\pi\) appears once on the left and twice on the right. Policy evaluation is the fixed-point problem \(\mathbf{v} = \mathsf{L}_\pi\mathbf{v}\), equivalently the root-finding problem \(\mathsf{L}_\pi\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{v} = 0\).

Rehearses: the three views of policy evaluation (linear system, iteration, Neumann series) on a small example.

Q16core2 min

Which statement about the Banach fixed-point theorem is correct?

  1. aAn operator that is not a contraction has no fixed point
  2. bA contraction has a fixed point, but successive approximation only finds it from a good initial guess
  3. cA contraction on a complete normed space has exactly one fixed point, and successive approximation converges to it from any starting point; the proof is the algorithm
  4. dContraction in the sup norm implies contraction, with the same factor, in every norm on \(\mathbb{R}^n\)

The condition is sufficient, not necessary: non-contractions may still have fixed points. The proof builds the Cauchy sequence \(x_{n+1} = \mathsf{T}(x_n)\), which is "an algorithm in disguise", and gives the a posteriori bound \(\|x_{n+1} - x^\star\| \le \frac{q}{1-q}\|x_{n+1} - x_n\|\). The contraction property is attached to a norm: \(\mathsf{L}_\pi\) contracts in \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\) but need not contract in a weighted \(\ell_2\) norm, which is one of the technical roots of the deadly triad.

E. Bellman optimality and value iteration the max, greedy policies, stopping rules

Q17core8 min, on paper

Two states, \(\gamma = 0.9\). In state 1 you can stay (reward 1, remain in 1) or go (reward 0, move to 2). State 2 has one action, stay (reward 2, remain in 2).

  1. Write the Bellman optimality equation and solve it for \(v^\star\). Which policy is optimal?
  2. Run value iteration from \(v_0 = 0\) for three sweeps. After each sweep, write the greedy action in state 1.
  3. What is wrong with the greedy policy after the first sweep, and why does it get fixed?
  4. With \(\varepsilon = 0.1\), the stopping test is \(\|v_{n+1} - v_n\|_\infty < \varepsilon(1-\gamma)/(2\gamma)\). About how many sweeps does it take? What does the test guarantee?

(a) \(v(2) = 2 + 0.9\,v(2)\), so \(v^\star(2) = 20\). \(v(1) = \max\{1 + 0.9\,v(1),\; 0 + 0.9\,v(2)\}\). Staying forever gives \(1/0.1 = 10\); going gives \(0.9 \cdot 20 = 18\). So \(v^\star = (18, 20)\) and the optimal policy is to go.

(b) \(v_1 = (\max(1, 0), 2) = (1, 2)\); greedy w.r.t. \(v_1\) in state 1: stay \(1 + 0.9 = 1.9\) versus go \(0.9 \cdot 2 = 1.8\), so stay. \(v_2 = (1.9, 3.8)\); greedy: stay \(2.71\) versus go \(3.42\), so go. \(v_3 = (3.42, 5.42)\); greedy: go.

(c) After one sweep, \(v_1\) only knows one step of reward, and the payoff of going (a delayed reward) has not propagated yet. Each sweep adds one step of lookahead; the greedy policy can be optimal long before the values are accurate, and here it is from the second sweep on.

(d) The threshold is \(0.1 \cdot 0.1/1.8 \approx 0.0056\). The change in state 2 is \(2 \cdot 0.9^{n}\) at sweep \(n+1\), which is the largest one, so we need \(2 \cdot 0.9^n < 0.0056\), \(n \ge 56\): about 57 sweeps. Then \(\|v_{n+1} - v^\star\|_\infty \le \varepsilon/2 = 0.05\) and the greedy policy is \(\varepsilon\)-optimal: its value is within 0.1 of \(v^\star\) in every state. (Running it gives \(v \approx (17.95, 19.95)\).)
